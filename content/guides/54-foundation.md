You can sell a house with foundation problems. You should not sell it by pretending the cracks are only paint. This guide is the order: see what the house is doing, write what you know, price the choice to repair or disclose, and then list. It is not an engineering opinion and it is not legal advice. Disclosure duties come from your state. In Texas, the seller-disclosure note points at the Texas Real Estate Commission form. The short notes cover whether you can sell, whether you must disclose, how to sell, and Texas in particular.

## What to look at before you call it a foundation

Walk the house the way the [Pre-listing walkthrough](/tips/walk-the-house-before-you-hire) describes, and write only what you can point at. Doors that suddenly stick, cracks in brick that have widened, floors that slope, and gaps at window frames are lines on a list. A hairline crack in a garage slab that has looked the same for years is a different line from a crack that grew this season. Write the difference. Do not diagnose pier failure from a blog photo.

Look at water next, because water and foundations travel together. Gutters that dump at the base of the wall, a grade that slopes toward the house, and a leak you have not traced belong on the same page. The prep guide's rule applies: stop active water before you pay to cover a symptom. [Water stain on ceiling selling house](/tips/trace-a-stain-to-its-source) is about stains. Use the same habit here. A foundation company and a plumber are different calls. Write which symptom you are asking each one to explain.

## A written opinion, then a price

If the list is more than a hairline you have watched for years, hire a structural engineer or another qualified inspector your state recognizes, and ask for a written report. A repair contractor's bid is not the same document as an engineer's letter. You may want both: one to say what is happening, and one to say what a repair would cost. A typical range for a structural engineer's visit and letter is often a few hundred dollars to around a thousand, and more if the house is large or the visit includes testing. A typical range for a foundation repair bid can run from a few thousand dollars for a limited area to much more when a whole side needs work. Those are labels so you know the size of the decision. The invoice and the bid in your city are the numbers you type into the [net proceeds calculator](/tools/net-proceeds).

Then choose. Repair before listing, credit the buyer, or sell with the report attached and a price that reflects the report. [Selling a house as is vs fixing up: how to decide](/tips/selling-as-is-vs-fixing-up) is the short version of that choice. "As is" does not mean "silent." It means you are not promising the repair. You still disclose what you know.

## Disclosure and the buyer's inspector

Write what you know on the disclosure form your state uses: the repairs you did, the reports you have, and the symptoms you have seen. Do not wait to see if the buyer's inspector finds the crack. Known facts belong on the form. The short note on disclosure is that duty. If you are in Texas, use the Texas note and the TREC seller's disclosure notice, and ask a Texas lawyer if you are unsure the form applies to this sale.

When a buyer asks for a repair or a credit, [Repair requests after inspection](/tips/ask-who-pays-for-requested-repairs) is the contract habit: who pays, and what "done" means. A foundation credit should be a dollar amount or a described scope, not "fix the foundation" with no drawing. Attach the report you already have so two inspectors are not describing two different cracks.

## Checklist

- Write each symptom you can point at, and note whether it has changed.
- Separate water problems from foundation symptoms before you hire.
- Get a written engineer's letter if the movement is more than a stable hairline.
- Get a repair bid that states the scope, not a doorway estimate.
- Type the bid or the credit into the net sheet before you pick a list price.
- Disclose known issues, prior repairs, and reports on the state form.
- Attach the report to the file the buyer will receive.
- Decide repair, credit, or as-is with disclosure, and write the decision.
- Answer any repair request with a scope and a payer, in the contract form.
- If you are in Texas, read the TREC disclosure note on this site before you sign the form.

## Worked examples

### Repair, then list

Suppose an engineer writes that one corner needs piers and a contractor bids $8,500 for that scope, with a start date three weeks out. Your pricing sheet currently assumes no repair. Subtract $8,500, or the bid you actually sign, before you publish a price. A typical range is not a substitute for the $8,500 bid. If the work will not finish before photos, either wait to shoot that side or disclose that the work is underway. Do not photograph a completed corner you have only scheduled. The listing-photo guide says to show repairs you finished.

### Credit instead of a repair

Suppose the same bid is $8,500 and you would rather close than manage a crew. You can price the house with the report in the file and offer a credit, or wait for the buyer to ask. If you offer $8,500 and the buyer wants $15,000, you are back to the net sheet and the bottom line from the pricing guide. Write the counter. Do not agree to "make it right" without a number. The offers guide is where that counter gets signed.

## Common mistakes

- Filling a crack the week before an inspection and saying nothing.
- Treating a contractor's sales visit as an engineering report.
- Using one national price as the cost of "a foundation."
- Writing "none" on a disclosure form when you have a report in a drawer.
- Letting a buyer and a seller use the word foundation to mean two different cracks.
- Starting a repair you did not agree to in the contract, then fighting about who pays.

## When to call a pro

Call a structural engineer when doors, slopes, or cracks are new or growing, or when a buyer will ask and you want a document that is not a sales pitch. Call a foundation contractor for a bid after you know the scope, and get two bids on the same scope if the first number surprises you. Call a real estate lawyer if a buyer claims you hid a report, or if you do not know whether your state's form requires a particular answer. In Texas, start with the TREC seller's disclosure notice and a Texas lawyer if the form's instructions do not match your facts.

Call a plumber or a drainage contractor when the symptom is water at the wall. Piers do not fix a gutter. You can make the written list yourself this weekend. The pro is the person who stamps or bids the work you cannot diagnose from the sidewalk.

## Questions

### Can I sell without repairing the foundation?

Yes. Buyers buy houses with known foundation work ahead of them when the price and the disclosure match the report. You cannot sell the silence. The short note on selling with foundation problems walks the contract side. This guide is the check you do first.

### What if I do not know whether the crack matters?

Say what you see, and hire the engineer if a buyer, an insurer, or your own doubt is large enough to need a letter. "I do not know" is a reason to get the letter. It is not a reason to answer "no" on a form that asks about known defects.

### Should I get the buyer's inspector to be the first person who looks?

You can, and you will negotiate from surprise. A pre-listing look, even a careful walk you do yourself, plus a report when the symptoms are real, lets you price the issue. The pre-listing inspection guide on this site is the broader version of hiring that look.

### Does a warranty replace disclosure?

No. A warranty is a product with exclusions. The disclosure is what you know. Offer a warranty only if you understand what it covers and you still tell the truth on the form.
