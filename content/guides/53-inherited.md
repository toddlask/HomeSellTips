This is not legal advice and it is not tax advice. A trust, a will, and a probate case are state documents. Federal tax on the sale starts with [IRS Publication 523](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523). Read it with a tax advisor, and ask a lawyer in the state where the house sits who is allowed to sign the deed. The short notes cover taxes, a trust after a death, a parent's house, a probate sale, and step-up in basis. This guide is the order of the first weeks.

Selling an inherited house, or one in a trust or estate, does not start with a list price. It starts with authority. Someone has to be allowed to sell. Until that person is named in a way a title company will accept, a buyer cannot get a deed they can rely on.

## Who is allowed to sign

Find the will, the trust, and any court paper already issued. The signer might be an executor, an administrator, or a trustee. Those are different jobs. A child who paid the utilities is not automatically the signer. A surviving spouse is not automatically the signer of a house that was only in the other spouse's name. Ask the title company, early, what letters or court orders they need before they will insure the sale. That question is free compared with a contract you cannot close.

If a court is supervising the estate, the sale may need the court's permission or a waiting period the local rules describe. The short note on a probate sale is that path. If a trust owns the house, the trustee sells under the trust's terms. Selling a house in a trust is not the same sentence as selling from a probate estate. Read the trust for who becomes trustee when someone dies, and whether a death certificate is enough or a court step remains. The short note on a trust after death stays on that difference.

## The people and the house, before the sign

Tell the insurer the owner died and ask what the policy covers while the house is empty. Keep the utilities on if the policy or the weather requires it. Change the locks if you are the person the lawyer says may do that, and do not change them against a co-owner who still has a right to enter. Secure valuables and mail. Do not throw away papers that show what the decedent paid for improvements. Publication 523 tells you the basis of an inherited home is a tax fact. The receipts still help a preparer, even when the starting basis is a date-of-death value.

Talk to the other heirs before you list, and write down what you agreed. A text thread is a poor substitute for a short signed direction if several people inherit. Disagreement about price is easier before a buyer is in the driveway.

## Basis, the exclusion, and the closing folder

Publication 523 says that if you inherited your home from a decedent who died before or after 2010, your basis is the fair market value of the property on the date of death, or the later alternate valuation date if the personal representative of the estate chose one. If a federal estate tax return was filed or required, the value listed there matters. A death in 2010 has its own discussion in the publication. Do not apply the sentence to a 2010 death without reading that part. The short note on step-up in basis is this rule in one task. An appraisal as of the date of death is the paper people wish they had ordered in the first month.

The main-home exclusion in Publication 523 is about the seller's own use of a main home. Heirs do not step into the decedent's two-year use test by default. Sometimes an heir who also lived there has their own tests to run. A surviving spouse has additional basis rules in the publication, and community-property states, including Texas, are called out there. A preparer applies those pages. This guide only tells you to bring them.

Open a folder: death certificate, will or trust, letters from the court if any, the deed, the loan number, insurance, and the date-of-death value. Then use the ordinary selling guides on this site for prep, price, and closing. Authority first. Paint second.

## Checklist

- Locate the will, the trust, and any probate papers before you call an agent.
- Ask a title company which signatures and court letters they need to insure the deed.
- Ask a lawyer in that state if the answer is unclear or if heirs disagree.
- List every heir and what each person has agreed, in writing.
- Tell the insurer and keep the house insured while it is empty.
- Save improvement receipts and order a date-of-death value for the basis file.
- Read the inherited-home basis section of Publication 523 with a tax advisor.
- Do not assume the main-home exclusion applies to the heirs.
- If a mortgage or a reverse mortgage remains, order a payoff quote before you set a price.
- Only then walk the prep guide and choose a list price.

## Worked examples

### Three siblings and a trustee

Suppose a parent's house is titled to a trust, and the trust names one child as successor trustee. That child may be the person who can list and sign, even if all three of them inherit the proceeds. The other two still need the trust's terms on who is told and how money is divided. A typical range for a lawyer to read a short trust and tell you who signs is often a few hundred dollars to more than a thousand, depending on the city and the mess. Get the fee in writing. The cost of listing first and discovering the trustee was never properly seated is a failed closing. These ranges are labels. The engagement letter is the price.

### A date-of-death value

Suppose the house was bought decades ago for $40,000 and an appraiser says the fair market value on the date of death was $310,000. Publication 523's inherited-basis rule, for a death before or after 2010, points at that $310,000 figure rather than the $40,000 purchase, subject to the publication's details and any alternate valuation date. If the heirs sell soon after for $315,000 and pay $20,000 of selling costs, the gain question is about the difference between the amount realized and the inherited basis, not about the 1970s purchase. A preparer computes it. The example only shows why the old purchase price is the wrong starting point. A death in 2010, a gift instead of an inheritance, or depreciation can change the file. Do not file from this paragraph.

## Common mistakes

- Listing the house because you are the child who lives nearest.
- Splitting furnishings while the signer is still unnamed.
- Using the decedent's old purchase price as basis without reading Publication 523.
- Assuming the parent's exclusion amount transfers to the heirs.
- Throwing out tax papers during the clean-out.
- Promising a buyer a closing date before the court or the trust will allow a deed.

## When to call a pro

Call a probate or trust lawyer when more than one person could claim the right to sell, when a court file is open, or when the trust is silent or disputed. Call a title company before you accept an offer, not after. Call a tax advisor with Publication 523 and the date-of-death appraisal before anyone spends the proceeds. Call the loan servicer if a mortgage or reverse mortgage is still on the house. The mortgage guide covers the quote. An estate does not erase the lien by itself.

You can gather the documents and secure the house once a lawyer tells you that you may. Do not change title, add an heir to the deed, or distribute cash ahead of the closing "to keep it simple." Those moves are how title gets worse.

## Questions

### Can I sell before probate is finished?

Sometimes, if the law and the title company allow a sale by an independent executor or a similar officer, and sometimes not. The probate-sale note is the question to take to a local lawyer. Do not advertise a date the court has not made possible.

### What if the house is in a living trust?

Selling a house in a trust depends on the trust. A trustee who already had authority may sell without a probate case. A trust that was never funded, so the deed is still in the decedent's name, may still need the estate. Read the deed and the trust together. The trust note walks that check.

### Do heirs owe capital gains tax?

They might, on gain above the basis Publication 523 describes, and they might not if the sale is near the date-of-death value and selling costs absorb the difference. There is also income-tax reporting to ask a preparer about. The exclusion for a main home is a separate test. Read the tax note and the publication. Do not divide a guessed tax by the number of siblings.

### Should we clean out the house before we know who signs?

Secure it, insure it, and stop there until the signer is clear. A full clean-out that throws away papers and offends a co-owner is hard to undo. Prep and staging come after authority, using the same guides as any other sale.
