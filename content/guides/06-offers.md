An offer is a stack of dates and dollars, not a single price. Read it in order, compare what you would keep, and answer in writing. The short notes are [How to read a purchase offer](/tips/read-the-whole-offer), [How to compare offers on a house](/tips/compare-net-and-dates), and [Put every deadline on one page](/tips/put-every-deadline-on-one-page).

## Read the whole stack before you react

Start with the names, the property address, and the price. Then read the closing date, the possession date, the financing, the earnest money, the contingencies, and what stays with the house. [How to read a purchase offer](/tips/read-the-whole-offer) is the short pass. A high price with a sale of the buyer's current house, a long inspection, and a request that you leave the refrigerator is not the same offer as a lower price with a two-week close and no extra personal property.

Write every deadline on one page: inspection end, loan commitment, appraisal, title objection, and the closing day. [Put every deadline on one page](/tips/put-every-deadline-on-one-page) is that sheet. If a date is blank, it is not a favor. It is a missing term. Ask for it in writing before you accept.

## Net, dates, and what you will not trade

Compare offers by the net you would receive and by the dates you can live with. [How to compare offers on a house](/tips/compare-net-and-dates) is the short version. Rerun the [net proceeds calculator](/tools/net-proceeds) for each set of credits and repairs. A price that is $5,000 higher and asks for an $8,000 credit is a lower net. The pricing guide tells you to keep the bottom line written down. Use it here. [Seller negotiation tips](/tips/write-what-you-will-not-negotiate) is the list of terms you already decided not to trade.

Split the price talk from the closing date. [Negotiating closing date](/tips/split-price-from-the-closing-date) keeps you from giving away a week of rent or a mover you cannot rebook just to win a small price move. [What stays with the house when you sell](/tips/list-what-stays-before-you-sign) should be settled before you sign, not remembered at the final walk. The washer you meant to take is a contract line, not a story.

## Answer in writing and keep the counters

Accept, reject, or counter in the way your contract requires, and keep a copy of every version. [How to counter offer on a house](/tips/answer-an-offer-in-writing) and [Counter offer records](/tips/save-every-counter-you-send) are the short notes. A text that says "we are good at 410" is not a counter if the form says the counter has to be signed. When a buyer later asks for repairs, [Repair requests after inspection](/tips/ask-who-pays-for-requested-repairs) is the question: who pays, and is it a credit or a completed job. Get the scope in writing. A promise to "fix the leak" without a description is how two people remember two different leaks.

The [CFPB closing guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/) and the [Closing Disclosure guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/) explain the papers that show up near the end. You are not at that page yet. You are making sure the offer you sign can survive until that page without a surprise credit.

## Checklist

- Read price, dates, financing, earnest money, contingencies, and included items before you celebrate.
- Put every deadline on one page and highlight any blank date.
- Rerun the net for each offer, including credits and repair requests.
- Check the offer against the bottom line and the terms you will not trade.
- Split a price change from a closing-date change.
- List what stays and what goes before you sign.
- Answer with the form your contract requires, not only a text.
- Save every counter you send and every one you receive.
- When repairs come up, write who pays and what "done" means.
- Stop and call your agent or attorney if a deadline is today and you do not understand a clause.

## Worked examples

### Two offers, one net

Suppose offer A is $420,000, asks for a $6,000 seller credit, and closes in 30 days. Offer B is $414,000, asks for no credit, and closes in 21 days. Before costs that are the same in both, A is ahead by $6,000 and then behind by the $6,000 credit, so the price-and-credit net is a tie. The difference is the date and whatever else is in the stack: financing, inspection length, and personal property. These dollars are an example. Replace them with the offers in your hand and with the costs from your own quotes. Do not pick A because the first line is larger.

If A also asks you to pay for a repair you priced at $2,500, A is no longer tied. [Net proceeds after repairs](/tips/rerun-the-net-after-each-bid) is the same habit from the pricing stage. Run it again. The sheet takes five minutes. The wrong signature lasts through closing.

### A counter that changes one thing

Suppose your bottom line is $400,000 net of the credit you are willing to give, and the offer is $405,000 with a $10,000 credit and a possession date a week after closing. You might counter the credit to $5,000 and accept the date, or you might counter the date and leave the credit. Do not move both in your head and write neither. [How to counter offer on a house](/tips/answer-an-offer-in-writing) says to answer in writing. Change the lines you mean to change. Save the signed copy. A phone call that "they are fine with it" still needs the form.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing offers by the purchase price alone.
- Missing a blank deadline or a contingency that lets the buyer walk late.
- Agreeing in a text and never signing the counter.
- Giving a possession week away without pricing the overlap.
- Promising a repair without a scope or a cost.
- Letting an included-items list contradict what you already moved out.

## When to call a pro

Call your agent or a real estate attorney before you sign an offer you do not understand, and any time a deadline is inside two days and a clause mentions default, forfeiture, or a repair cap. Ask the question in writing so the answer is in the file. Call the title company when earnest money instructions name an account you were not expecting. Do not wire a deposit to a number that arrived only by email. The closing guide's wire rule starts now: call a known number.

You can compare nets yourself. The calculator does the arithmetic. The pro is for the clause you cannot explain in a sentence, and for the form your state requires. Bring the deadline sheet so the meeting is about the blank line, not a reread of the whole stack from zero.

## Questions

### Do I have to take the highest price?

No. You can prefer a lower net that closes when your next house closes, or reject a high price that depends on the buyer selling another home. Write the reason in your file. The other party gets your signed answer, not your diary.

### What if two offers arrive the same day?

Put both on the deadline sheet and both through the net sheet before you counter either. A fast counter on the first one can collide with the second. If your agent is collecting them, ask when the review window ends. Get that time in writing too.

### Can I accept and then change the closing date?

Only by a signed change. A verbal shift is how movers get booked for the wrong day. [Negotiating closing date](/tips/split-price-from-the-closing-date) and the moving guide both assume the date in the contract is the date you tell the truck.

### Who pays for repairs the inspector writes up?

Whoever the signed response says. It might be you, the buyer, a credit, or nobody because you refuse. [Repair requests after inspection](/tips/ask-who-pays-for-requested-repairs) is the short question. Do not start the work until the response is signed and the scope is specific.
