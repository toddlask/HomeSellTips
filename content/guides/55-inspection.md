A pre-listing home inspection is a paid look at the house before you advertise, so you can repair, disclose, or price what the inspector writes down. It is optional on an ordinary sale. It is not the same task as walking the house yourself. Do the walk first. The [Pre-listing walkthrough](/tips/walk-the-house-before-you-hire) is that walk: one line per fault, marked fix, disclose, or leave. This guide is the decision to hire someone after that list exists. The short notes define the inspection, its cost, a checklist, and whether it is worth the fee.

## What you already know, and what you are buying

Your walk finds the drip, the sticky door, and the stain. An inspector is a second pair of eyes with a method and a report. You are buying the report, not a promise that the house will pass a buyer's later inspection. A buyer's inspector can still disagree, still test different systems, and still write a longer list. Plan for that. The value of your report is time: you see the list while you still control the calendar, not on day eight of a contract with a deadline.

Ask what the fee includes before you book. A general inspection is not a roof certification, a sewer scope, a pest letter, a pool inspection, or an engineering letter on the foundation. Those are extra visits with extra fees. If your own walk already shows a foundation symptom, the foundation guide comes before you pay a generalist to tell you to hire an engineer.

## How to use the report

Sort the report the same way you sorted your walk. Fix what is cheap and true, or what will dominate a buyer's first hour. Disclose what you will not fix. Leave cosmetic notes you have decided not to buy, and do not pretend they were repaired. Put any repair bid into the [net proceeds calculator](/tools/net-proceeds) before you set the list price. [Selling a house as is vs fixing up: how to decide](/tips/selling-as-is-vs-fixing-up) is the short choice. [What to fix before selling a house](/tips/fix-function-before-finish) is the order: function before finish.

Decide whether you will give the report to buyers. If you give it, give it complete. Handing over the cheerful pages and filing the rest is how a disclosure fight starts. If your state form asks what you know, the report is something you know. The disclosure notes in the foundation and Texas topics are about honesty. This topic is about the inspection itself. Ask your agent or a local attorney if you are unsure whether a pre-listing report must be delivered. Do not shop for an answer that says to hide it.

## Timing

Order the inspection after the obvious repairs you already planned, or before them if you want the inspector to help you rank the list. Do not order it for the morning the photographer arrives. Do not order it so late that a repair you choose will miss the list date. A useful report arrives while you can still change the price or the scope. A report that arrives after the offer is signed is just an early version of the buyer's inspection, and you paid for the surprise yourself.

If you are selling without an agent, you still can hire the inspector. The agent-or-FSBO guide is about who schedules the visit and who answers questions about the report. Put a name on that line.

## Checklist

- Finish the do-it-yourself walk and mark fix, disclose, or leave.
- Ask the inspector what the fee includes and which specialists it does not.
- Book a date that leaves time to repair or reprice before you list.
- Attend the inspection so you hear the comments that never make the summary page.
- Sort the report into fix, disclose, or leave, in writing.
- Get bids for anything you might repair, and put the bids in the net sheet.
- Decide if the full report goes to buyers, and do not edit out pages.
- Disclose what you now know on the form your state uses.
- Keep the report in the closing folder with the repair receipts.
- Expect the buyer's inspector to write a second list. Do not promise they will not.

## Worked examples

### A general inspection and two specialists

Suppose a general pre-listing inspection is quoted at $450. Your walk already noted a damp basement corner and a roof with missing tabs. The inspector's quote does not include a sewer scope or a roofer. A typical range for a general home inspection is often about $300 to $600, with higher quotes for large houses or markets where the visit runs long. A sewer scope or a roof certification is a separate typical range, often about $150 to $400 each when a specialist offers a simple visit. These are labels. Your two or three written quotes are the budget. If you only buy the $450 visit, you still do not know the sewer. Write that limit in your notes so you do not tell a buyer the house was "fully inspected."

### Spending the fee to avoid a bad week

Suppose the report flags a double-tapped breaker and an active tub leak. An electrician's written bid is $350 and a plumber's bid is $600. You fix both, disclose that you did, and list. The alternative is a buyer's inspection deadline and a credit fight in the offers guide. The inspection fee plus $950 of bids is the cost of handling it on your calendar. It is not a guarantee the next inspector shrugs. It is a smaller surprise. Type $450 plus the bids into the net sheet. If you decline the repairs, disclose the report instead of filing it.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping your own walk and expecting the inspector to be your list.
- Calling a general inspection a roof, sewer, pest, and foundation clearance.
- Scheduling the visit the day of the photos.
- Giving buyers a partial report.
- Repairing cosmetic notes and ignoring a leak the report put on page one.
- Promising a buyer that their inspector will find nothing because you already paid one.

## When to call a pro

Call a home inspector when you want a written method and you will use the result to repair, disclose, or reprice. Call a specialist when the general report or your own eyes point at the roof, the sewer, the chimney, a pool, or the foundation. Call an engineer for structure, as the foundation guide describes. Call your agent or a lawyer if you are unsure whether to release the report. Call nobody extra if the house is simple, your walk is clean, and you are willing to negotiate whatever a buyer later finds. That is a valid choice. Write it down so it was a choice.

Ask the inspector about their license or registration if your state requires one. Ask for a sample report before you pay. A verbal walk with no document is a conversation. You paid for a file you can disclose.

## Questions

### Will a pre-listing inspection replace the buyer's inspection?

No. Many buyers will still hire their own person, and their lender may require an appraisal that is a different visit entirely. Your report helps you. It does not bind them.

### Should I fix everything in the report?

No. Sort it. Function and known safety issues come before worn finishes. Disclose what you leave. [What to fix before selling a house](/tips/fix-function-before-finish) is the order. A 40-page report is a sorting job, not a work order you must finish.

### Is it worth it on a newer house?

Sometimes the fee buys a short report and peace of mind, and sometimes your own walk is enough. The worth-it note compares the fee with the size of the surprises you are trying to move forward. There is no rule that a newer house skips the visit or that an older house always needs one.

### Can I do the inspection myself and skip the fee?

You can do the walk. You should. That is the linked walkthrough, and it is not a licensed inspection. If you want a report with a name on it, hire the inspector. Do not call your notepad an inspection when a buyer asks what you had done.
