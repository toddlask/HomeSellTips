This is not tax advice. Your return depends on facts this page does not have. Read [IRS Publication 523](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523) and [IRS Topic No. 701, Sale of your home](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc701), then ask a tax advisor before you file. The short notes under this topic each do one job. This guide is the order of the ideas.

Capital gains tax when selling a house is a federal question about gain, not about the size of the check the title company wires. Gain is not the sale price. Publication 523 is the IRS publication that explains the rules when you sell your home, including an exclusion that can keep some or all of the gain on a main home off your income if you qualify.

## Price, basis, and gain

Start with the amount realized. That is the sale price minus selling expenses the publication tells you to subtract, such as commissions you actually paid. Then subtract your basis. Basis starts with what you paid, and it changes when you add capital improvements or subtract items the publication lists, including depreciation you were allowed to claim. The result is your gain or loss. If you skip the basis file and subtract only the mortgage payoff, you are mixing a loan with a tax figure. The payoff matters at the closing table. It is not your basis.

[IRS Topic No. 701](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc701) states the headline exclusion in plain words: if you have a capital gain from the sale of your main home, you may qualify to exclude up to $250,000 of that gain from your income, or up to $500,000 of that gain if you file a joint return with your spouse. Publication 523 provides the rules and the worksheets. Those dollar caps are the publication's figures, not a promise that your sale qualifies.

## The ownership and use tests

Topic 701 explains the tests in general terms. You meet the ownership test if you owned the home for at least 24 months (2 years) out of the 5 years ending on the date of sale. You meet the use test if you lived in it as your main home for at least 24 months of that same 5-year period. You can meet the two tests during different 2-year stretches, but both stretches have to fall inside those 5 years. Generally you are not eligible for the exclusion if you excluded gain from the sale of another home during the two-year period before this sale.

A partial exclusion can apply in some situations the publication lists, and it is smaller than the full cap. Do not invent a partial amount. Use the publication's worksheet with a preparer if you moved for a reason you hope qualifies. Depreciation you claimed after the date the publication discusses can remain taxable even when the rest of the gain is excluded. That is a line for the preparer, not a sentence to guess.

## A second home, a rental, and the records

A house that was never your main home does not get the main-home exclusion just because you owned it. A house that was your main home and later a rental can be split between qualified use and other use. Publication 523 explains nonqualified use and depreciation. The short note on a second home is the place for that narrower question. Bring the dates you moved in and out, not a summary from memory.

Keep the records that show basis and the sale. Publication 523 has a section on what records to keep: purchase papers, improvement receipts, and the closing statement. The IRS page [How long should I keep records?](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records) explains how long to keep records after you dispose of property. Read it for the period that applies to the year of the sale. Do not adopt a round number of years from a conversation. The short note on records walks that file. Keep the copies even after the wire arrives. The wire is not the worksheet.

## Checklist

- Read Publication 523 and Topic 701 before you treat the wire as spendable.
- Write the sale price and the selling costs the publication allows you to subtract.
- Find the purchase file: price, closing costs that became basis, and improvement receipts.
- List any depreciation you claimed on this house.
- Mark the dates you owned it and the dates it was your main home, inside the 5-year window.
- Note whether you excluded gain on another home in the prior two years.
- If the house was a rental or a second home, flag that for a preparer instead of assuming the full exclusion.
- Save the closing statement with the basis file.
- Ask a tax advisor before you file, and before you spend money you may owe.
- Ignore any calculator on the internet that does not ask for basis.

## Worked examples

### A main home inside the tests

Suppose you bought a house for $200,000, added a room that was a capital improvement at a written cost of $40,000, and sold it years later for $400,000. Selling costs on the statement are $24,000. A rough gain is 400,000 minus 24,000 minus 240,000, or $136,000. If you meet the ownership and use tests and you qualify to exclude up to $250,000, that illustration sits under the cap Topic 701 describes. These figures are an example, not your return. They ignore depreciation, a partial exclusion, and any state tax. A preparer still runs the worksheet. The point of the example is that the gain is not $400,000 and it is not the cash you took home after the mortgage.

### A rental stretch in the middle

Suppose you lived in the house, rented it, and moved back, and the dates only partly fill the use test. Do not apply the $250,000 figure and stop. Publication 523 walks nonqualified use and depreciation recapture with worksheets. Bring the lease dates and the depreciation you actually deducted. A typical range does not belong on a tax worksheet. The numbers are yours. The short note on a second home is the companion page. This example only shows why the main-home story and the rental story are different jobs.

## Common mistakes

- Calling the sale price, or the cash after payoff, your taxable gain.
- Assuming every seller gets $250,000 off without the ownership and use tests.
- Forgetting a prior exclusion inside the last two years.
- Dropping depreciation you claimed on a home office or a rental period.
- Throwing away improvement receipts after closing because the buyer has moved in.
- Using a friend's exclusion amount from a joint return on a return that is not joint.

## When to call a pro

Call a tax advisor before you file if the house was a rental, a second home, an inheritance, or a home you received in a divorce, or if you claimed depreciation. Call them if the gain might be over the exclusion, or if you are not sure you meet the 24-month tests. Bring Publication 523, the closing statement, and the basis file. Ask for a written list of anything still missing.

You can organize the dates and the receipts yourself. That prep saves the appointment. It does not replace the signature on the return. If someone offers to "erase" the gain for a fee without reading the publication, walk away and use the IRS pages linked here.

## Questions

### Do I owe capital gains tax if I buy another house?

Publication 523 taxes the sale you made. Buying the next house is a new basis file. It does not automatically cancel gain on the one you sold. Older rollover rules are not the current exclusion. Read the current publication, not a memory of a rule from decades ago.

### What if I lived there one year?

You may fail the full use test and still fit a partial exclusion the publication describes, or you may not. Do not scale $250,000 by the months in your head. Use the worksheet with a preparer. The ownership test is separate from the use test.

### Are improvements the same as repairs?

Publication 523 treats capital improvements, which add to basis, differently from repairs, which do not. A new roof that is a capital improvement belongs in the basis file with the invoice. A patched leak you expensed may not. Ask the preparer with the invoice in hand. This page will not reclassify your receipts.

### Does this cover state tax?

No. Publication 523 is federal. Your state may tax the same sale differently, or not at all. Ask a preparer who handles returns in the state where you file. Bring the same folder.
